small-model-lab · Part 2 · Post-training

From a toy to a tool

In Part 1 we built a tiny GPT from scratch and hit its ceiling: it learned the shape of language but never anything useful. This chapter is the other half of the arc: how real models are made useful. We take a pretrained open model and fine-tune it into TrialScout: a small expert that turns an oncology clinical-trial record into a structured, investor-relevant readout, running on a laptop, for free.

The whole idea: a giant model already knows language; we don't re-teach that. We just specialise it for one narrow, structured task, by showing it ~1,500 worked examples and nudging a tiny set of its parameters. The result beats a sensible baseline by a mile and nearly matches the expensive model that taught it.

And none of this is new machinery. The model is the same animal you built in Part 1 — a transformer, attention blocks and all, just ~1,200× bigger — and fine-tuning is the same training loop (tokens, the loss, gradient descent), now in service of something that does a job.

How to read this. Same as Part 1: the idea first, then the real code, then a plain-English gloss. Part 2 is more pipeline than algorithm, so the code boxes show the excerpts that do the real work (pulled straight from the repo) rather than every line. Haven't read Part 1 yet? Start there; this chapter assumes its vocabulary.

Stage 1 · Orientation

0

The shift: don't build, adapt

Part 1 ended on an honest ceiling: a from-scratch model, trained on a laptop, produces believable words but no real knowledge or reasoning. Closing that gap from scratch would take what the giants spent: trillions of tokens, thousands of GPU-hours. So we don't.

Instead we start from a model that has already paid that price: a pretrained open model (here, Alibaba's Qwen3-4B, 4 billion parameters, freely downloadable). It already knows English, clinical jargon, JSON, the lot. Our job is narrow: specialise it for one task it isn't yet reliable at: reading a trial record and emitting a clean, structured readout. That's post-training, and it's how almost every useful model you've met was made.

Why a small model can win here

A 4B model will never be a great general analyst, but it can be excellent at one narrow, structured task. Trial-record → fixed-schema-JSON is exactly that shape: bounded inputs, a controlled vocabulary out. Get it right and you have an expert that runs locally, costs nothing per call, needs no internet, and answers in seconds: things a frontier API can't all offer at once.

Stage 2 · Teaching the model the task

1

The task, and the contract

First, the domain, for anyone new to it. A clinical trial is a study that tests whether a treatment (here, a cancer drug) is safe and actually works. Drug companies run thousands of them, and to an investor each one is a potential catalyst: an upcoming result that can move a company's value. The trouble is the raw information: every trial is written up in long, inconsistent prose, and there are tens of thousands of them. So TrialScout's job is to turn each trial into a short, uniform structured summary, so a whole population of trials becomes something you can scan, filter, compare, and screen at a glance, instead of reading registry pages one at a time.

Concretely: every trial has a public record on ClinicalTrials.gov, the US government registry of studies. Each record is semi-structured data (a title, phase, status, lead sponsor, the conditions and drugs under test, the primary outcome measure, key dates, enrollment, trial design) plus free text. We fetch it from the registry's API and trim it to the ~20 fields that matter: that trimmed record is TrialScout's input. Its output is a compact ten-field JSON readout: nct_id, phase, indication, intervention_class (is this even a drug trial?), modalities (what kinds of drug, as a list), primary_endpoint_type, sponsor_type, est_readout (its expected readout as a half-year, e.g. “H2 2026”), risk_flags, and a ≤2-sentence investor_note. Most output fields are enums (a fixed menu of allowed values), so the readouts are directly comparable across trials, and easy to score.

JSON, in thirty seconds

If the term is new to you: JSON is the plain-text format most software uses to pass structured data around. (The name stands for JavaScript Object Notation, but nothing here involves JavaScript; the format long ago outgrew its origin.) A JSON object is just "name": value pairs inside curly braces — {"phase": "Phase 2", "enrollment": 41} — where a value can be text, a number, true/false, a list, or another object nested inside. That's the whole format. It's everywhere because both sides can read it: a person can eyeball it, and a program can check that every field is present and valid — exactly what we'll need when we start scoring outputs. The two boxes below are both JSON: a trial record in, a readout out.

The work is turning verbose, inconsistent registry data into normalised judgement: reading “Merck Sharp & Dohme LLC” and writing large pharma, or a raw 2021-06-08 completion date into H1 2021. That's why it needs a model, not a parser. Here is a real held-out trial (NCT03631407), with TrialScout's actual output:

what this says

What this is: a JSON Schema, the contract for the model's output. Each enum (modality, primary_endpoint_type, sponsor_type, risk_flags) is a closed list of allowed strings. Constraining the output this way does two jobs at once: it makes a small model's task tractable (pick from a menu, don't free-write), and it lets us measure correctness later by simple comparison, with no human grading the bulk of it.

Structured-in, structured-out

This shape (a messy record in, a strict object out) is where small models genuinely beat the “just ask a chatbot” approach: it's cheap, deterministic enough to trust, and trivially pluggable into a spreadsheet or a pipeline. It's also why the same recipe transfers to any extraction task (SEC filings, lab reports, support tickets), not just trials.

2

Distillation: a strong model writes the textbook

To fine-tune the student we need a pile of correct examples (1,500 here): trial in, perfect readout out. Writing those by hand would take an analyst weeks. So we distill: a strong, expensive model (Claude Sonnet 5) reads each trial and writes the gold answer, and the small student then learns to reproduce those answers. The teacher's judgement becomes the student's training data.

Step back and name it: this is plain supervised learning, training on labeled (input → correct output) pairs. Part 1 was self-supervised: the next character was a free label already sitting in the text, so nobody had to annotate anything. Here there is no free label; we must supply the right readout for each trial. (Done to an already-pretrained model, this is called supervised fine-tuning.) The only unusual part is where the labels come from: not human annotators, but a stronger model. Training a small model on a big model's outputs is knowledge distillation.

what this says

The key moves:

  • tool_choice={"type": "tool", "name": "emit_readout"}: forced tool-use. The teacher can't free-write; it must call the emit_readout tool, whose arguments are the schema. So every answer comes back already valid and parseable, with no JSON wrangling.
  • temperature=0: deterministic, no creative drift; we want the teacher's single best call.
  • system=SYS: the rules + worked examples, marked prompt-cached so the big static prefix is billed once, not per trial. That (plus a cap and a pilot gate) kept the whole labeling run to about $14.
Teacher quality caps student quality

This is distillation's iron rule: the student can only get as good as the answers it's shown. A weak teacher silently ceilings the whole project, so we pay for a strong one (Sonnet), not a cheap one. The student will end up near the teacher, never above it. (We'll see that ceiling bite, hard, in §8.)

Automating a job that used to be manual

The slow, expensive part of supervised learning has always been getting the labels: a human reading each example and hand-writing the answer. Labeling 1,500 trials that way would take an analyst weeks. Distillation collapses it to a few hours and about $14: the teacher does the tedious annotation. Cheap, fast, scalable labeled data is one of the biggest unlocks in modern ML, and the same move works for any structured-extraction task, not just trials.

3

The data pipeline (and not burning money)

Generating labels with a paid API, unattended, demands guardrails. The labeling script fetches trials from the free ClinicalTrials.gov API, then calls the teacher with three safety nets: a hard cost cap, a 10-trial pilot gate (abort before the bulk run if quality is poor), and resumable writes (a crash loses nothing).

what this says

What this says: label the first 10 trials, count how many come back schema-valid, and if fewer than 8 pass, stop before spending on the other 1,490: a broken prompt fails cheap, not at full cost. The bulk loop (not shown) adds the hard dollar cap and appends each result to disk as it arrives. The clean gold is then split 80/10/10 into train / validation / test sets.

Why the held-out test set matters

That final 10% (150 trials the model never trains on) is the only honest measure of whether it learned the task versus memorised the training set. Every score in §6 is on this held-out set. Keeping it frozen is what makes the numbers trustworthy.

Stage 3 · Fine-tuning

4

LoRA: a small patch on a big model

Now the fine-tune. The naïve way (update all 4 billion of Qwen's parameters) needs far more memory than a laptop has, and risks the model forgetting everything else it knows. The standard fix is LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation): freeze the entire base model and train only a small set of extra “adapter” weights that sit alongside it. You learn a tiny patch, not a new model.

Two questions worth answering before the command. Where does the adapter come from? It's created, not chosen: when the run starts, the trainer bolts small blank matrices alongside the frozen weights, initialised so they have zero effect, and gradient descent writes into them — they are the only parameters allowed to move. The 28 MB file this run produces is the adapter; it didn't exist before training, and on its own it's useless — it only means something snapped onto its base model. And is there a menu of adapters to pick from? Not for a new task. What you pick are its dials: how many of the model's layers get a patch (we adapt the top 16) and how wide each patch is (the rank, which we leave at the trainer's default). Ready-made adapters do get shared around for existing tasks — a downloadable “LoRA” for an image model is exactly this kind of file — but nobody has trained one for trial readouts, which is rather the point of this chapter.

what this says

Reading the flags: --fine-tune-type lora picks the adapter approach; --num-layers 16 attaches adapters to the top 16 layers; --iters 700, --batch-size 4, --learning-rate 1e-4 are the same training-loop dials from Part 1 (steps, batch, step-size): gradient descent, unchanged, just pointed at this task. --mask-prompt means the loss is computed only on the answer tokens, not the trial text, so the model learns to produce readouts, not echo inputs.

The result: train loss fell 2.56 → 0.16 in ~50 minutes on the Mac, producing a 28 MB adapter. That's the whole trained model: a small delta you apply on top of the public base.

5

Evaluation: the part that makes it real

In Part 1 we judged the model by reading its output: fine for “does this look like English?”, useless for “is this correct?”. A useful model needs a real scorecard. So the eval harness is a first-class deliverable, not an afterthought: it runs the student over the 150 held-out trials and scores each field against the gold answer.

what this says

What this says: load(model, adapter_path=...) loads the frozen base and snaps the LoRA adapter on top (that's how LoRA is consumed). For each trial it builds the same prompt used in training, generates text, pulls the first {…} object out, and snaps any near-miss enum value to the closest legal one (so eval matches what the deployed server does). score() then compares the seven scoreable fields to gold (accuracy and macro-F1 for the four single-value enums, exact match for est_readout, and set-F1 for the two list fields, modalities and risk_flags; F1 is a 0–1 score balancing false positives against misses, 1.0 perfect), exactly the metrics the baseline was measured with, so the comparison is fair.

Two kinds of grading

One field is no longer asked of the model at all. Seven of the eleven risk flags are arithmetic on numbers already in the record — small enrollment (<50) is literally enrollment < 50. The teacher got that comparison wrong 20 times in 150, and the student faithfully learned the mistake. Those seven are now computed by the pipeline; the model is asked only for the four that need reading the trial.

Seven structured fields grade themselves: the four single-value enums by string match, est_readout by exact match, and the two list fields (modalities, risk_flags) by set overlap. Free text can't: investor_note is scored by Claude-as-judge (a separate model call rates the note for faithfulness), and indication, free text too, isn't auto-scored at all. Automatic where possible, model-graded where necessary.

Stage 4 · The result

6

The result

Here is the payoff, on 1,444 trials the model never saw — a fresh, randomly-sampled held-out set, not the small one we started with. Four comparisons matter, and they say different things. The majority-class baseline (always guess the most common value for each field) is the floor. Untuned Qwen is the same open model on the same prompt before any fine-tuning, told the list of allowed values but nothing else. The frontier model is Claude Sonnet 5 — the expensive model that wrote the training labels — handed the schema and nothing else, no rules and no worked examples. That last column is the one that makes the headline honest: it is the bar a small local model has to clear.

Why the untuned column needs a footnote

Asked with the bare training prompt, untuned Qwen scores 0.121 — far below the 0.476 floor. It isn’t incapable; it has simply never been told the menu. The training prompt names the fields but never lists their allowed values, because the student absorbs those from 1,192 worked examples. So the base model answers other to almost everything and hands back a raw date where the schema wants “H1 2029”.

Paste the allowed values into the prompt and the same model jumps to 0.697. That single change is worth +0.576 — more than everything fine-tuning adds afterwards. It is the most useful number on this page: most of what looks like “the small model can’t do this” is really “nobody told it what the answers may be.” The table quotes the fair version, 0.697, so the fine-tuning gain isn’t flattered.

What this number means

0.70 → 0.94, against a 0.48 floor — and past the frontier model’s 0.90. A 4-billion-parameter model, fine-tuned for an hour on a laptop and run for free, scores higher on this task than the frontier model that taught it, when both are handed the same schema and scored the same way. That is the whole thesis of the chapter, measured.

Now the caveat that has to travel with it. The gold answers were written by that frontier model with a page of decision rules and worked examples. The student trained on those conventions; the frontier column never saw them. So the honest sentence is “fine-tuning beats prompting, on the conventions the teacher was given” — not “a 4B model is smarter than a frontier model.” The gap is widest exactly where conventions live: est_readout, a mechanical date rule, 0.980 against 0.827.

And the win runs out on the tail. Weighting every modality equally instead of by how often it appears, the student scores 0.653 and the frontier model 0.805. On rare drug classes the frontier model is far better: antibody-drug conjugates 1.00 against the student’s 0.40, oncolytic viruses 1.00 against 0.33. Distillation transferred the conventions and not the pharmacology — 1,192 examples contain only a handful of each rare class, and a model cannot learn from what it has barely seen. The student wins the average and loses the long tail, which is exactly where a specialist reader would notice.

7

Decide by eval, not by faith

Which base model to fine-tune, Qwen3-4B or Google's Gemma 4 E2B? We didn't argue about it; we ran both through the same harness and let the score decide. That's the discipline: model choice is a measurement, not a preference.

In the end Qwen won by default and by margin. Gemma 4 E2B failed to train at all: the only available checkpoint is a multimodal (vision+text) build whose weights the LoRA trainer couldn't target. And Qwen's score is so near the ceiling that even a perfectly-trained Gemma would have to beat it to flip the call, implausible on this task. So the measured decision is Qwen, robustly.

A failed arm is still a result

“Gemma didn't train” isn't a gap in the write-up; it's a finding (and a real-world gotcha: check whether a checkpoint is text-only before you plan around it). The honest version of an A/B includes the arm that didn't work and why it doesn't change the conclusion.

8

Can you beat the teacher? (The error-mining loop)

The weakest field was modality. The natural next move is a recursive improvement loop, a general pattern worth naming: mine the model's errors → generate targeted new training data for exactly those cases → retrain → measure → repeat, each pass trying to climb a little higher. It's the honest, buildable kernel of the “self-improving AI” idea. We ran one turn of it: snapped near-miss enum values for free, then added 300 fresh gold examples of the rare modalities the model kept missing.

It barely moved: a statistical wash. We concluded the residual errors were label noise — cases where the teacher's own answers disagreed — and that you can't loop your way out of that with more data.

That conclusion was half wrong, and finding out why is the most useful thing in this chapter. Going back through the errors months later, the biggest cluster wasn't the model being unsure. It was our schema being unanswerable. The modality field offered ten real modalities plus combination — which is not a modality at all, but a statement about how many there are. Nearly six errors in ten sat on that seam, running in both directions, because the question had no stable answer. Worse: one oncology trial in six tests no drug at all — a surgical technique, a radiotherapy schedule, a device — and the schema forced a drug modality onto every one of them.

So we rebuilt the field: modalities became a list (a trial pairing an antibody with chemotherapy says both), combination was deleted, and a new intervention_class field lets a trial answer “there is no drug here.” Then we regenerated every label and retrained from scratch. The “how many” argument fell from 59% of modality errors to 36%. Two-thirds of what we had written off as irreducible noise was our own specification, and it was fixable.

What remains is the other half of the original diagnosis: rarity. The scorecard said the model was still missing antibody-drug conjugates (0.40) and oncolytic viruses (0.33), so we bought 300 more examples of exactly those classes, retrained, and measured again. Nothing moved. Both figures came back identical, to two decimal places.

They came back identical because the test set could not see the difference. Those 150 held-out trials contain 5 antibody-drug conjugates and 3 oncolytic viruses. One trial is worth 20 and 33 recall points. “0.40” was two trials out of five. We had built a scorecard that was excellent for a headline number and blind to precisely the thing we were trying to fix — and we had already drawn a confident conclusion from it once before.

So we built a second, larger test set weighted toward the rare classes — 402 trials, 45 per modality. On that one the extra data had worked all along. But that set had its own flaw, and it is worth seeing: we had found those trials by searching for the class name, and a search for “bispecific antibody” can only return trials that say “bispecific antibody”. It was stacked with the easy cases and could not, by construction, contain the hard ones. Two test sets, two different biases, both confidently wrong.

The third attempt was the boring one that worked: 1,444 fresh trials, sampled at random, no enrichment, matching the real mix to within a percentage point. On that, the model reads antibody-drug conjugates at 0.91 and bispecifics at 0.79. Not 0.40. Not 0.77. The model had been fine the whole time and we had measured it three ways before getting one worth trusting.

Check that your ruler can measure the thing

Before running an experiment to move a number, work out the smallest change your measurement could detect, and compare it to the change you are hoping for. If one test example is worth 20 points and you expect a gain of 10, the result is decided before you start — and the answer you get back is a confident zero, which looks exactly like the intervention not working.

This project made that mistake twice, months apart, and both times wrote a thoughtful explanation of why the extra data hadn't helped. Neither explanation was true. Then the fix for it — a test set enriched by searching for each rare class — introduced a second, opposite bias, and produced a number that was too flattering instead of too harsh.

The part worth remembering: the small test set was perfectly good at its actual job. Its overall score, 0.936, sits within noise of the 1,444-trial answer, 0.932. It was only ever unreliable per category, where five examples decide a number. A cheap measurement can be trustworthy in aggregate and worthless in detail, and the two failure modes look identical until you check.

Three ceilings, and only two are real

(1) You can't beat your teacher — a distilled student asymptotes toward the model that taught it, not past it. Real. (2) You can't out-data label noise: once the remaining errors are cases the teacher itself answers inconsistently, more examples just teach the inconsistency. Also real — the teacher agrees with itself only about 70% of the time on risk_flags, so that field's practical ceiling is well under 1.0.

(3) “The category is just ambiguous” — often not real, and the most comfortable place to stop. It sounds like humility and it looks like a ceiling, but here it was a specification we had written badly and could simply rewrite. The check that separates the two: when a model contradicts itself at a category boundary, test whether your own taxonomy drew that boundary before concluding the model is weak. A genuinely self-improving system would need a cheap, non-gameable verifier of truth. Fixing your own schema is the part you can do today.

Stage 5 · Ship it

9

Packaging it as a callable expert

A trained adapter on disk isn't useful yet. The last step makes TrialScout callable: wrapped as an MCP tool (Model Context Protocol, the standard way to expose a tool to an AI client like Claude). Now any assistant can ask it for a readout by trial ID, and it answers locally, no API spend.

what this says

What this says: the @mcp.tool decorator registers trial_readout as a callable tool with a typed input (an NCT id, validated by a regex) and a choice of markdown or JSON output. Under the hood it fetches the trial from ClinicalTrials.gov, runs the fine-tuned model locally, and validates the result against the schema before returning it. The annotations (readOnlyHint, idempotentHint) tell the calling assistant it's a safe, repeatable read. This is the “callable expert” the whole pipeline was for.

10

The arc, closed: running it yourself

That's the full arc. Part 1 built a language model from nothing to understand the machinery; Part 2 took a pretrained one and specialised it into a measurably useful expert, for the price of some patience and about $14 of teacher calls. The giants are not different in kind; they are this, at scale. And the techniques here (distillation, LoRA, eval-driven decisions) are exactly how a small team turns a big open model into something that does their job.

Read-along vs. run-it-yourself

Unlike Part 1, this chapter is mostly read-along: the gold dataset and the trained adapter are gitignored (regenerable, not committed), and step 2 spends ~$14 of Anthropic API to recreate the labels. Everything you need to reproduce it is above and in the repo, but you can absolutely just read it and take the method with you.

Where this goes next

The honest finding (§8) points at the real frontier: a model that improves itself needs a verifier of truth it can't game, not just more teacher labels. TrialScout proved the method. That harder idea is where this lab is headed, but the chapter isn't written yet: for now, the arc ends here.
